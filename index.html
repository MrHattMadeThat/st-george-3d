    About this stage

    An illustrated historical landscape of St. George in the Granite Town years (1874–76), made as a stage for short animations about the granite trade: quarry, canal, river, sheds, wharf and ship.

    How it was made

    • Shorelines, lakes, rivers and marshes come from OpenStreetMap. Hills come from the AWS Terrain Tiles elevation model. Both are modern and have been simplified.
    • Hills are drawn taller than life (the “Hill height” slider) so the land reads at a glance.
    • Farms and clearings are drawn only around settlements of the 1870s; everything else is forest, as it mostly was.
    • The red granite belt and its ledges follow Gwen Martin’s Maps 2 and 3.
    • The town, farms and villages are drawn with simple 1870s-style houses, barns and stores. They show how the place was laid out, not particular buildings. Streets are the old core streets named on Martin’s Map 4, following today’s lines.
    • The Bay of Fundy Red Granite Co. mill, its dam and flume, the upper and lower bridges and the main wharf are placed from Martin’s Map 4. Their shapes are imagined.
    • The Saint George Red Granite Co. quarry tramway (1873) runs from the Front (Burpee) quarries down to the company’s first finishing shed (Martin, p. 10, p. 13 and Map 3). Its course is drawn by eye; the shed opened in February 1874 and burned in June 1874. There is no mainline railway: the Grand Southern reached St. George only in 1880.

    Known differences from 1874

    • The Gulley is reconstructed as dry ground before the 1902 pulp mill dam. Terrain and shorelines remain approximate.
    • The Gorge, upper and lower falls, bridges and mill follow Martin’s Map 4. The map combines several periods; later industrial buildings are not all present in the 1874 scene.
    • Names marked * are placed by eye from maps or written descriptions. Churches stand at today’s church sites; their 1874 sites are not confirmed.
    • The people are illustrations of the work the sources describe: quarrymen drilling rows of holes and splitting blocks, a horse derrick, stonecutters at their bankers, teamsters, polishers and shippers (O’Halloran 1968), and the everyday life of a small town. They are not particular people.
    • The Stone’s Journey follows the Bay of Fundy company’s way of working as Martin describes it. The sleds, shear legs, stone truck, rollers, skids and tackle that move the stone are the ordinary tools of the trade, shown to explain how it could have been done, not recorded details.
